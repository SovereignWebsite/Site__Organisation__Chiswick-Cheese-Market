Lucy’s November 2026 Cheese of the Month: Tête de Moine

Tête de Moine is a cylindrical, washed, semi-soft, raw cow milk cheese weighing just under a kilo. It has a fine-grained texture that melts in the mouth, and the milk is from the lush spring pastures of the mountains of the Bernese-Jura region which gives it a distinctive Alpine  flavour. It is matured for a minimum of 10 weeks on pine boards in one of only 8 registered Tête de Moine dairies.

First made by the monks who arrived in the 12th century ‘Tête de Moine’ means ‘Monk’s head’ and there are many historical references to the monks paying for the use of land and buildings with their cheese. Its name may simply have been a ‘head count’ and reference for amount paid rather than a derisory term referring to its likeness to a monk’s shaved head!

Most commonly we see this cheese being sold in rosettes or ‘flowers’ made by shredding the cheese very thinly with a ‘Girolle’ (a sharp blade that rotates and shaves very thin slices in a circular motion resulting in flower-like bundles). In fact, this machine was not invented until 1981 so you most certainly do not need one of these to enjoy this cheese.  Tête de Moine won its first cheese medals at the Paris World Exhibition as early 1856 – so it has been enjoyed for far longer than the Girolle has been around!

So, what does it taste like? It is a firm dense texture like its Alpine counterparts but for me it has a deeper richer flavour even at 2 ½ months – its less fruity and sweeter, yeastier and more savoury than young Comté or a June made Beaufort and the washed rind is most definitely more pungent on the nose. Because this has both a deep texture and flavour (which gets deeper and richer as it matures) I enjoy a crisp, minerally white wine with it,  but if I have it on a cheeseboard after a meal,  I would equally enjoy it with a Riesling or if I wanted red, a Chianti with its cherry notes.

This is, however, one of my favourite cooking cheeses – it’s intense savouriness adds real umami to almost any dish and it pairs really well with autumn flavours – I add it to mushroom risotto rather than parmesan – try grating it, stirring it with a little mascarpone to make a thick paste to top crusty bread and make a quite incredible cheese on toast!  I also love to use the shredded ‘flowers’ piled high to top bruschetta. This is much smaller in size than its Alpine cousins but what it does not have in size, it makes up for in flavour as it packs a powerful, pungent punch on the palate. Alpine cheese lovers – you will not be disappointed!

Alfie @europeanfinecheese stall will be selling the cheese cut or shaving its distinctive flowers using a traditional girolle machine. Don’t miss their competition this month to win your very own Girolle machine.
